What Simulone can do

Simulone is the engine under every game here: thousands of simple agents, each choosing what to do next from its own needs, on a grid world the player shapes. It is plain JavaScript with no dependencies and no build step, made for phones first. Everything that makes one game different lives in that game's own files.

How an agent decides

Every few ticks each agent scores every action it may take and does the best one. The score is its situation multiplied by its type's matrix of weights, so behaviour is data, not code.

The engine

What any game gets from engine/. Tags show which games use a feature most.

Agents and their needs

  • Needs rise every tick; the squared value makes urgent ones dominate. A need can be capped so it is a want, never an emergency.
  • Actions answer needs at a building, at home, at work, at a tile, at a point, or on the spot. They end when the need is met or after a set time.
  • Types with their own speeds, need rates and weights; every agent also varies a little from the rest.
  • Ages blend the matrix smoothly from child to elder. Lord's WillColony
  • Interruptible actions: a sleeper wakes for a desperate need; needs build slowly while asleep.
engine README: Needs, Actions, Types, Age

Buildings and places

  • Solid footprints with a door and an optional separate exit, turned to face a street.
  • Capacity and queues: a full place makes people wait outside, then give up for a while.
  • Homes and jobs handed out automatically, nearest first, within a commute; a place with no worker stays shut.
  • Dragged buildings such as fields, sized by the player, with work in proportion. Lord's Will
  • Door linger and staying in for the next thing: short visits never blink in and out of sight.
  • Closing a place for a game's own reasons (fire, debt), with each reason kept apart.
engine README: Prefabs, Homes and jobs, Views

Maps and movement

  • Free movement in any direction along distance fields, with crowds pushing apart.
  • Streets that are cheaper and faster, so people prefer them; idle people stroll along them.
  • Dug-out maps that start solid and are carved open. Colony
  • Links between distant cells: stairs, lifts, escalators. Atrium
  • A game's own routes ("the nearest cell to dig"), cells to avoid, and crowded cells that slow everyone.
  • Points that move every tick: a soldier keeps his slot in a marching line. WAR
  • Routes recomputed in the background after every edit, without stopping play.
engine README: Tiles, Dug-out maps, Routes, Points

Drawing

  • WebGL2, every sprite in one draw call, sorted by a depth buffer.
  • Isometric and flat views built in, or a view of the game's own. Colony
  • Smooth motion between ticks at any frame rate; time runs at any speed or pauses.
  • Building images chosen by how full they are; rooms seen inside with furniture people pass behind. Atrium
  • Varied looks per person, walking and doing poses, building states (on fire), night tint with lit windows that glow.
  • Ground textures, one part of the map at a time (a floor of a building).
engine README: Views, Sprites

The player's hands

  • Build mode freezes time while the map is edited.
  • Placing in two steps: a ghost to drag, turn and check before it is built, sized for a finger.
  • Tap to pick a person, a building or a cell; lay lines of tiles; demolish.
  • A game may refuse a building, with a reason to show (not unlocked, cannot pay).
engine README: Build mode, Placing in two steps, Game object

Room for a game's own rules

  • Modules that hook into spawning, every tick, starting and finishing actions, leaving, building and more.
  • Per-person values of the game's own (money, fear, health), added and removed during play.
  • Inputs to the matrix of the game's own: an order, being poor.
  • Headless: the simulation runs in Node with no browser, for tests and balance runs.
engine README: Your own rules, Headless

Each game's own modules

Written generically too, but kept with their game until a second game needs one. Then it moves to the shared modules.

The Lord's Will

A medieval hamlet grown into a chartered town.

  • region: several maps under one game, with time passing on all of them
  • ambience: a soundscape made in the browser, by season, hour and view

Atrium

A shopping mall of up to five floors.

  • floors: levels side by side, joined by stairs, lifts and escalators
  • visitors, staff: people who come and go, and shifts
  • trade, reputation, upkeep: sales, a slow rating, wear and repairs

The Colony

An ant nest seen in a cut through the earth.

  • ground, tasks, items: digging, posted work, carrying
  • zones, claims: rooms painted by the player, land claimed
  • scent, water, weather, foes: trails, floods, rain and raids

WAR

A campaign of provinces and battles of thousands.

  • regiments: formations and orders
  • combat, morale: blows, arrows, fear, rout
  • general, campaign: an enemy commander, and the war map

Around the engine

The screen

  • The shared kit gives every game its top strip, bottom bar, sheets, tray, cards, toasts and full-screen moments, laid out for a phone's safe areas, in light and dark.
  • A game restyles it by redefining its colours.

  • Aseprite scripts that draw all the art, one style guide per game.

Known limits

  • A place stays open while its workers are away eating or sleeping: staffing is by who is assigned, not who is there.
  • A queue is a loose group at the door, not an ordered line.
  • Jobs further than a commute from home are not taken, so a map can show vacancies and jobless people at once.
  • The map's size is fixed when a game starts; very large maps paint their ground at reduced detail.
  • Sprites are hard-edged: no partial transparency.
